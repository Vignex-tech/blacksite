# BLACKSITE master brief (owner's original prompt)

Shared by the owner on 2026-10-20. The full content is below, with long bullet lists joined into sentences. Where it conflicts with `CLAUDE.md`, `CLAUDE.md` wins. The main differences:
- The project is a browser game (Vite, TypeScript, Babylon.js, Colyseus), not Unreal Engine 5. The rendering features named here (Nanite, Lumen, Niagara and so on) map to browser equivalents within the first-load budget.
- Ghosts carry no gun (owner, 2026-10-20). They kill only by a sneak grab from behind and a second interact press. A grabbed Warden can struggle free, or a teammate can free them by shooting the Ghost.
- The current order (art pass, then the Ghost takedown and crouch, then the two-device test) is in `CLAUDE.md`.

MASTER AI GAME DEVELOPMENT PROMPT
PROJECT: BLACKSITE — ASYMMETRIC TACTICAL STEALTH MULTIPLAYER

1. YOUR ROLE
Act as a senior multiplayer game director, Unreal Engine 5 gameplay programmer, network engineer, technical artist, character animator, level designer, sound designer, UI/UX designer, and gameplay systems architect.
Your task is to design and build a fully playable online asymmetric multiplayer stealth-action game inspired by the design philosophy of classic spies-versus-mercenaries games.
Do not create a generic military FPS with a stealth character added to it. Create a competitive multiplayer experience in which two opposing teams have fundamentally different gameplay systems, movement capabilities, camera perspectives, sensory information, and combat roles.
The final game must prioritize:
- Asymmetric gameplay that creates meaningful decisions.
- Tension generated by darkness, sound, uncertainty, and player intelligence.
- Fluid third-person spy movement against deliberate first-person mercenary movement.
- Vertical traversal, environmental stealth, ambushes, hacking, and hostage interactions.
- Skill-based gunplay and counterplay.
- Stable online multiplayer with server-authoritative gameplay.
- High-quality cinematic graphics without sacrificing competitive performance.
Use original intellectual property. Do not copy proprietary characters, logos, maps, sound effects, animations, or extracted assets from existing franchises.
Working title: BLACKSITE: Shadow Protocol
Genre: Asymmetric tactical stealth, first-person shooter, third-person parkour, objective-based multiplayer.
Target experience: PC-first, keyboard and mouse, controller support designed into the input architecture.
Recommended engine: Unreal Engine 5, using C++, Blueprints, Enhanced Input, dedicated servers, and replicated gameplay systems.

2. CORE GAMEPLAY CONCEPT
Create two teams with completely different perspectives and capabilities.
Team A — Ghosts: elite infiltration specialists. They use third-person movement, parkour, climbing, stealth, hacking, silent takedowns, vertical traversal, and information gathering. They are physically vulnerable to direct gunfire. They cannot win conventional firefights against prepared defenders.
Team B — Wardens: heavily equipped security operators. They use first-person weapons, flashlights, tactical equipment, defensive gadgets, and sound-based tracking. They control the facility, protect sensitive information, and hunt infiltrators. They must coordinate their patrols and defend multiple routes simultaneously.
Primary match objective: Ghosts must infiltrate a secure facility and extract classified data from protected terminals. Wardens must prevent data extraction until the round timer expires or eliminate the attacking team.
Use a default 2v2 mode, with an architecture that can later support 3v3 and 4v4.
A standard match should last approximately 6–10 minutes, with configurable timers for testing and competitive balancing.
The game must be tense even when neither team sees the other. Players should constantly interpret footsteps, lights, shadows, alarms, movement, and objective activity.

3. NON-NEGOTIABLE GAME DESIGN RULES
- Ghosts are not simply weaker soldiers. Their advantage comes from mobility, concealment, information, and positioning.
- Wardens must not have permanent omniscient vision of Ghosts.
- Darkness must influence visibility without making the game impossible to read.
- Flashlights must be useful but can reveal the Warden's position.
- Every powerful mechanic must have a counter.
- Ghosts must be able to climb, hang, traverse, and exploit vertical geometry intentionally designed into the map.
- A hostage grab must create a tense interaction rather than an automatic victory.
- The game must remain understandable to spectators and new players.
- Both teams must have distinct sound design, camera behavior, and user-interface information.
- Networking, latency handling, and anti-cheat architecture must be considered from the beginning, not added after the game is complete.

4. VISUAL DIRECTION AND GRAPHICAL QUALITY
Create a grounded, cinematic, near-future industrial espionage aesthetic. The visual identity should combine:
- Contemporary military-industrial architecture.
- High-security corporate research facilities.
- Concrete service corridors, metal maintenance structures, ventilation ducts, server rooms, laboratories, and security checkpoints.
- Deep shadows punctuated by practical lights, emergency indicators, monitors, and occasional exterior illumination.
- Realistic proportions, physically based materials, believable wear, and controlled visual effects.
Avoid exaggerated science-fiction armor, excessively bright neon, unrealistic weapons, and visually noisy environments. The result should feel like a believable place that has been converted into a secure tactical battleground.

4.1 Rendering technology
Use Unreal Engine 5 features where appropriate: Nanite for suitable high-detail static geometry; Lumen for dynamic global illumination and reflections, with performance alternatives; Virtual Shadow Maps or another tested shadow solution; Physically Based Rendering materials; Temporal Super Resolution or equivalent upscaling options; Niagara for particles and environmental effects; hardware-accelerated ray tracing as an optional setting, not a mandatory requirement; level streaming and World Partition where the map scale justifies them; distance-based level of detail and material complexity management.
Do not enable every expensive rendering feature by default. Establish measurable frame-rate targets and create scalable graphics presets.
For a first playable PC build, target 60 FPS or better on the minimum supported hardware, with higher frame rates available on stronger systems. Select the exact hardware baseline before final optimization.

4.2 Color palette
Use a restrained, readable palette:
- Charcoal black: deep structural shadows.
- Cold blue-gray: metal, concrete, and ambient illumination.
- Muted green: security equipment and limited surveillance indicators.
- Warm amber: maintenance lighting and selected interior fixtures.
- Emergency red: alarms, critical warnings, and restricted access.
- Neutral white: high-security lighting and task illumination.
Team colors must not be the sole way to distinguish players. Use silhouettes, equipment design, interface indicators, and contextual information without allowing wall-penetrating team outlines.

4.3 Contrast and readability
Design lighting so that a player can distinguish: a completely hidden Ghost; a Ghost moving through partial illumination; a Ghost silhouetted against a brighter background; a Ghost exposed directly to a Warden's flashlight; a Warden visible through a doorway; a Warden's flashlight beam moving across a wall; a temporarily disabled light; a recently disturbed environmental object.
Darkness must not be equivalent to rendering a player invisible under all conditions. The visibility model should consider illumination, surface exposure, distance, viewing direction, movement, background contrast, and visual effects. Gameplay readability takes priority over physically accurate darkness when the two conflict.

5. TEXTURES AND MATERIAL SYSTEM
Create a consistent PBR material library. Use original materials or appropriately licensed assets. Every major surface must have believable roughness, reflectivity, texture scale, and physical response to lighting.

5.1 Concrete
Variants: smooth poured; rough structural; painted; damp; cracked and chipped; with dust accumulation; with old water stains; around ventilation outlets and utility installations.
Texture maps: base color; normal map; roughness; ambient occlusion where appropriate; optional height or displacement detail for close-up surfaces.
Use subtle roughness variation, localized stains, small edge chips, and believable surface imperfections. Avoid repetitive tiled patterns and excessive grunge.

5.2 Metal
Separate materials for: brushed steel; painted industrial steel; galvanized metal; stainless steel; blackened weapon-grade metal; powder-coated security panels; rusted maintenance hardware; anodized equipment components.
Metal must respond convincingly to flashlight illumination. Use metallic values appropriate to the physical material, roughness variation, fine scratches, fingerprints where suitable, edge wear, and controlled reflection intensity. Do not make every metal object mirror-like.

5.3 Glass
Support: security glass; frosted glass; reinforced observation windows; transparent partitions; dirty and scratched glass; broken glass where destruction is supported.
Implement believable transparency, reflections, refraction where practical, and appropriate rendering order. Glass must not create unfair visibility advantages. Test how both teams perceive players through each glass type.

5.4 Fabric, skin, and character equipment
Ghost clothing should include technical fabrics, flexible armor panels, reinforced knees, gloves, boots, utility straps, and a compact climbing harness.
Warden equipment should include ballistic fabric, protective plates, helmet components, radios, weapon accessories, and practical flashlight mounts.
Use: fabric normal detail; stitching and seams; material-specific roughness; subtle wear; skin shading with appropriate subsurface scattering where supported; physically plausible metal and polymer components.
Avoid unnecessary high-frequency detail that creates visual noise at competitive viewing distances.

5.5 Dynamic material effects
Implement material parameters for: wet surfaces; water droplets; flashlight illumination; emergency lighting; holographic or electronic display elements where appropriate; temporary power failures; small scorch marks and damage decals; dust and dirt accumulation where performance allows.
Dynamic damage should be limited and intentional. Do not require full destructibility of every environment object.

6. ENVIRONMENT AND LEVEL DESIGN
Create the first map: BLACKSITE — Research Annex 07. The map is a compact, multi-level classified research facility. It must be designed around the unique movement and sensory abilities of both teams.

6.1 Required areas
A. Security lobby: a central entrance; security desks; reinforced doors; partial cover; bright and dark transition zones; clear lines of sight for Wardens; alternative upper traversal routes for Ghosts.
B. Server room: tall server racks; narrow aisles; blinking status LEDs; cable trays; floor grilles; multiple hacking terminals; localized equipment hum; strong vertical shadows.
C. Maintenance corridors: narrow passages; pipework; utility panels; flickering lights; interconnected service routes; opportunities to bypass conventional doorways.
D. Laboratory: observation windows; workbenches; glass partitions; movable cover; security doors; at least two distinct infiltration approaches.
E. Upper service catwalk: elevated walkways; railings; maintenance platforms; overhead beams; sightlines into selected lower rooms; drop-down ambush positions.
F. Ventilation and overhead traversal: designed climbing anchors; wall-mounted pipes; structural ledges; narrow maintenance passages; overhead hanging points; a limited number of routes connecting separate rooms.
Do not let players climb arbitrary surfaces without authored traversal support. Build explicit traversal volumes, anchor points, collision rules, and animation alignment.
G. Objective control room: two possible data terminals; several entrances; defensible but not impregnable geometry; nearby alternate routes; strong environmental audio; a readable approach to the objective.

6.2 Vertical gameplay
Every important combat area should offer a deliberate combination of: ground-level routes; elevated routes; overhead hiding positions; doorway approaches; sightline breaks; at least one meaningful alternate route.
Ghosts should be able to reach certain locations that Wardens cannot reach directly. Wardens should compensate through surveillance, positioning, defensive gadgets, and coordinated coverage.
Do not create endless ceiling routes that make Wardens helpless. Every traversal route should have a strategic trade-off.

6.3 Lighting layout
Place practical lights rather than relying on a uniform global illumination setup. Examples: ceiling fluorescent fixtures; emergency strip lighting; desk lamps; small server LEDs; wall-mounted maintenance lights; motion-triggered lights; backup power lighting; flashlight illumination.
Implement a controlled number of lights that can be disabled, damaged, or temporarily interrupted. Lighting changes must replicate consistently across clients.

6.4 Environmental interaction
Implement selected interactions: doors that open, close, and lock; terminals that can be hacked; light switches and electrical panels; security cameras; motion sensors; breakable light fixtures where supported; ziplines between authored anchors; climbable structural elements; movable or interactive cover in limited locations; alarms and objective warning systems.
All gameplay-relevant interactions must be validated by the server.

7. TEAM A: GHOST CHARACTER SYSTEM
Ghosts use a third-person over-the-shoulder camera with an emphasis on movement, awareness, and spatial positioning. Their movement should feel fluid, responsive, athletic, and deliberate.

7.1 Character appearance
Create an original, grounded infiltration operator with: dark technical clothing; low-profile protective armor; climbing gloves; reinforced boots; a compact equipment harness; a covered or partially concealed face; a small number of visible electronic devices; equipment that moves believably with animation.
Character proportions should be realistic. Avoid oversized armor and exaggerated superhero movement. Support cosmetic customization without providing competitive advantages.

7.2 Third-person camera
Implement: smooth over-the-shoulder camera movement; mouse-controlled horizontal and vertical look; configurable camera sensitivity; camera collision and obstruction handling; controlled shoulder swapping; context-sensitive camera offsets; camera behavior for crouching, climbing, hanging, and takedowns; optional field-of-view settings within fair competitive limits.
The camera must not permit excessive peeking around corners without exposing the character. Prevent the camera from clipping through walls, revealing hidden opponents, or seeing through geometry.

7.3 Movement states
Implement a movement state machine with: idle; walking; fast movement; crouching; crouch-walking; sprinting; jumping; falling; landing; vaulting; mantling; ledge hanging; shimmying along a ledge; wall-assisted movement; climbing; ceiling hanging; zipline traversal; ladder traversal; interaction; stealth takedown; hostage interaction; stunned or incapacitated; death and spectator states.
Each state must define valid transitions, collision behavior, movement speed, noise generation, camera response, and replication rules.

7.4 Animation quality
Use a high-quality locomotion animation system. Required: idle breathing; start and stop animations; smooth directional locomotion; eight-direction movement blending where appropriate; crouch locomotion; sprint acceleration and deceleration; foot placement and foot IK; hand IK for climbing and interaction; root-motion vaulting where appropriate; motion warping for alignment with traversal anchors; procedural body lean; upper-body aiming or tool use; landing reactions based on fall height; smooth transitions between grounded and traversal states.
The character must never appear to slide over the floor, snap abruptly between states, or float away from ledges. Use motion matching if the animation budget and technical requirements justify it; otherwise, a well-authored animation blueprint and blend spaces are sufficient.

7.5 Climbing and ledge traversal
The player can: detect valid climbable surfaces; jump toward a valid ledge; grab the ledge; hang with both hands; move laterally; climb upward; drop safely; mantle onto the platform; transition to an overhead hanging position where an authored connection exists.
Traversal detection must use server-validated geometry and predictable client-side presentation. Prevent climbing through walls, traversing outside the map, attaching to invalid collision surfaces, and exploiting level seams. Use authored traversal anchors for cinematic actions and procedural alignment for simpler movement.

7.6 Signature ceiling ambush: Shadow Drop
The Ghost approaches a supported overhead anchor, performs a controlled jump or traversal action, and attaches to the overhead surface. While hanging: movement speed is reduced; the character's hands and feet align with the surface; camera movement remains smooth; the Ghost can observe the area below; the Ghost produces small movement sounds if repositioning; a mercenary passing underneath may become vulnerable to a contextual takedown.
To perform the takedown: the Ghost must be in a valid overhead anchor state; the target must be within a defined interaction volume; the target must satisfy orientation and state requirements; the server validates range, line of sight, and the action; a synchronized animation plays; the Warden is incapacitated or eliminated according to the selected mode.
The ability must be counterable through awareness, positioning, defensive equipment, and team coordination.

7.7 Stealth and visibility
Ghosts should benefit from: remaining still in deep shadow; using cover; avoiding direct light; moving slowly through noisy spaces; taking alternate routes; distracting Wardens; breaking line of sight.
Create a server-authoritative visibility evaluation system with client-side presentation. Use lighting probes, visibility zones, line-of-sight checks, distance, silhouette contrast, movement, and flashlight illumination. Do not implement an arbitrary invisibility toggle. The game must clearly communicate when a Ghost is exposed without providing perfect hidden-player tracking to the opposing team.

7.8 Ghost gadgets
Signal Tap: temporarily disables or disrupts one nearby eligible surveillance device; produces a subtle interaction sound; has a cooldown.
Noise Decoy: creates a localized sound that imitates plausible movement or an environmental disturbance; does not reproduce exact player location information; can be investigated by Wardens.
Pulse Scanner: provides limited information about nearby electronic devices; does not automatically reveal enemies through walls; has a short duration and cooldown.
Gadgets must use server-validated charges, cooldowns, and interaction rules.

8. TEAM B: WARDEN CHARACTER SYSTEM
Wardens use a first-person perspective. Their experience should feel deliberate, tactical, heavy, and information-driven. They are powerful when defending a position but vulnerable to misdirection, flanking, and vertical ambushes.

8.1 First-person rendering
Weapon and hands visible in first person; independent first-person arms where appropriate; full-body third-person character for other players; correct synchronization between first-person and third-person actions; weapon collision handling; camera recoil; aim-down-sights transitions; reload animations; flashlight and gadget animations. Avoid visible camera clipping into walls or the character model.

8.2 Movement
Wardens can: walk; sprint; crouch; aim; reload; interact; use gadgets; push through selected doors; recover from a stagger; perform defensive hostage interactions; jump over low obstacles where permitted.
Wardens cannot use the full Ghost climbing system. They should move through stairs, ladders, doors, and designed access routes.

8.3 Weapon system
Start with one primary weapon, one sidearm, and one melee or defensive interaction. Implement: server-authoritative hitscan firing; configurable fire rate; recoil; spread; reloading; magazine management; aim-down-sights; muzzle flashes; shell ejection; impact effects; penetration rules for selected materials; weapon audio variations based on the environment; friendly-fire configuration for testing.
Do not make every wall penetrable. Establish material-specific penetration rules and verify them on the server. Use restrained, readable recoil rather than random screen shake.

8.4 Flashlight mechanics
First-person mounted flashlight; third-person flashlight representation; real-time beam direction; adjustable beam width; falloff and attenuation; optional focused mode; battery or heat limitations only if playtesting shows they improve balance; reflections on suitable surfaces; volumetric beam effects when appropriate; surface response based on material roughness; replicated on/off state.
A flashlight can expose a Ghost but also reveal the Warden's position. Avoid overly expensive dynamic shadows from every flashlight. Use a tested lighting budget and scalable shadow settings.

8.5 Sound detection
Distinct sounds for: running footsteps; slow footsteps; crouched movement; landings; metal surfaces; concrete surfaces; glass; water; door movement; ventilation; hacking; climbing; zipline traversal; gadget activation; weapon fire; environmental alarms.
Sound attenuation should account for distance, occlusion, room geometry, material differences, and background noise. Do not give Wardens a perfect wallhack disguised as sound detection. Sound cues should indicate plausible direction and approximate distance, with uncertainty. Support stereo and headphone spatialization, with accessible audio settings.

8.6 Warden defensive gadgets
Motion Sensor: covers a limited area; detects qualifying movement within its designed field; provides an alert rather than a permanent enemy marker; can be disabled or bypassed.
Portable Floodlight: illuminates a selected area; helps reveal concealed movement; can be avoided or disabled through an appropriate interaction.
Security Camera: provides a restricted surveillance view; has a defined field of view; can be disrupted by Ghost equipment; requires the operator to spend time using the camera.
Each gadget must have deployment limits, clear audio or visual feedback, and counterplay.

9. HOSTAGE GRAB AND VOICE INTERACTION
Signature feature; dedicated design and testing time. Interaction name: Hostage Control. A Ghost who successfully approaches an eligible Warden from behind can initiate a grab.

9.1 Grab validation
Require: a defined maximum range; a suitable relative orientation; a valid line of sight; an eligible Warden state; a server-approved interaction; a short, synchronized animation transition. Do not allow instant grabs through walls, across excessive distances, or during incompatible animations.

9.2 Hostage animation
The Ghost pulls the Warden into a controlled restraint. Animate: Ghost arm placement; Warden defensive reaction; head and torso positioning; hand contact; foot placement; weapon lowering or disabling; small body adjustments; escape attempts; release and takedown transitions. Use animation warping to align the characters. Maintain consistent collision and root positions across the network. The hostage must remain a physically coherent interaction rather than two characters playing unrelated animations.

9.3 Hostage gameplay
While restrained: the Warden's normal firing is disabled; the Ghost's movement is limited; the Ghost can communicate; the Ghost may negotiate or taunt; the other Warden can attempt a rescue; the hostage can attempt a counteraction if the mode permits; the Ghost can release the hostage or perform a contextual takedown.
A teammate's rescue shot must be possible only under fair, clearly readable conditions. The hostage must not be immune to every form of damage, nor should a single grab automatically guarantee an elimination.
State machine: approaching, grabbing, restraining, negotiating, escape attempt, rescue, release, takedown, and interrupted interaction.

9.4 Voice filters
Modular voice processing with optional presets: radio distortion; low-pitched cinematic filter; high-pitched cartoon-like filter; narrow-band communications; mild robotic modulation; pitch-shifted character voice. Filters apply to player-selected voice audio, not generated from the player's identity.
Push-to-talk by default. Include microphone testing, mute controls, volume controls, and clear voice privacy settings. If using proximity voice chat, transmit the voice only to players permitted by the current interaction and game rules. The Ghost should be able to speak to the hostage through a contextually appropriate voice channel. The hostage's teammate may hear the exchange only if the chosen rules allow it. Support an alternative text or quick-chat system. Do not require a paid external voice service for the core game to function.

10. OBJECTIVES AND MATCH FLOW
10.1 Ghost objective: enter the facility; identify or approach an eligible data terminal; initiate the data extraction interaction; defend the interaction window or interrupt Warden countermeasures; complete extraction; reach a valid extraction point or satisfy the selected objective completion rule. Use at least two potential objective locations.
10.2 Warden objective: deploy to defend the facility; identify suspicious sounds and disturbances; maintain coverage of key approaches; interrupt or contest terminal access; use defensive equipment; eliminate or delay Ghosts until the timer expires. Wardens receive objective alerts, not exact information about Ghost positions.
10.3 Match states: main menu; lobby; team assignment; loadout selection; ready check; match countdown; round active; objective interaction; player elimination; round resolution; scoreboard; rematch; return to lobby; disconnect and reconnect handling. Configurable round duration, objective interaction time, gadget cooldowns, and match scoring. Track wins, losses, objectives completed, successful defenses, eliminations, assists, and other meaningful statistics.

11. ANIMATION AND VISUAL EFFECTS
Every action must have a clear beginning, active phase, and completion.
11.1 Character animation library (create or source properly licensed): idle breathing; walking and running; crouching; sprint starts and stops; turning in place; jumping; falling; landing; vaulting; mantling; ledge grabbing; ledge shimmying; ceiling traversal; zipline use; ladder climbing; weapon aiming; firing; reloading; weapon switching; gadget deployment; terminal interaction; door interaction; hostage grabbing; hostage restraint; hostage rescue; takedowns; hit reactions; death; spectator transitions. Use a consistent skeleton wherever practical. Retarget carefully and validate hand placement, foot contact, root motion, and collision.
11.2 Animation polish: state machines; blend spaces; aim offsets; inertial blending; motion warping; IK for feet and hands; turn-in-place; upper-body layering; procedural head and torso orientation; camera response that does not interfere with aiming. Brief anticipation for major movement actions; no unnecessary delays to responsive controls.
11.3 VFX: muzzle flashes; weapon impact sparks; dust particles; footstep dust; subtle landing particles; electrical sparks; hacking interface effects; light fixture failure effects; small glass break effects; environmental smoke; flashlight dust illumination; objective completion effects; restrained damage indicators. Sensible particle budgets, distance culling, and reduced effects settings. Visual effects must not obscure players or create unintended tracking information.

12. SOUND DESIGN AND AUDIO MIX
Audio is a primary gameplay system. Layered soundscape: low ventilation rumble; electrical equipment hum; server fan noise; distant machinery; occasional structural creaks; fluorescent light buzz; door mechanisms; footsteps; clothing movement; equipment rattles; weapon handling; flashlight switches; hacking sounds; alarms; voice communications.
Distinct footstep material sets and environmental reverb. Footsteps change according to speed, stance, surface, and movement action. Occlusion and attenuation so players can infer approximate location without perfect positional data.
Different mixes for: Ghost gameplay; Warden gameplay; hostage interactions; spectator mode; menus. Important gameplay audio audible over ambience. Separate sliders for master, effects, ambience, voice, and music. Avoid loudness manipulation that would be unfair across audio equipment.

13. USER INTERFACE AND UX
Clean, tactical interface that does not clutter the dark environment.
13.1 Main menu: Play; Find Match; Create Private Lobby; Training; Loadout; Customization; Settings; Credits; Exit. Animated background scenes, subtle environmental motion, restrained lighting, clear navigation.
13.2 In-match HUD. Ghost: objective status; interaction progress; gadget charges and cooldowns; teammate status; relevant sound or detection feedback; hostage interaction state; match timer. Warden: ammunition; weapon state; gadget status; objective timer; teammate status; relevant sensor alerts; interaction progress; hostage rescue state. No permanent enemy markers.
13.3 Interaction prompts, only when relevant and in range: hack terminal; disable camera; grab ledge; climb; drop; attach to zipline; use ladder; deploy gadget; rescue teammate; escape restraint. Contextual controls and progress feedback.
13.4 Accessibility: adjustable sensitivity; remappable keyboard and controller controls; adjustable subtitles; color-vision-friendly indicators; adjustable motion blur; camera shake controls; flashing-light reduction; audio mix controls; push-to-talk configuration; UI scaling; toggle and hold interaction alternatives where feasible.

14. ONLINE MULTIPLAYER ARCHITECTURE
Multiplayer is mandatory. Do not simulate an online match with two local characters. Dedicated authoritative server architecture for competitive matches: server-authoritative movement validation, damage and objective interactions; replicated match state; client prediction for responsive movement where appropriate; server reconciliation and interpolation; secure account and session integration; dedicated server deployment.
14.1 Replicate: player positions and movement states; team assignments; health and elimination state; weapon actions; gadget states; doors and interactive objects; objective progress; light states; surveillance devices; traversal states; hostage interactions; match timers; round results. Replicate only the information a client needs. Do not send hidden Ghost positions or secret gameplay states to opposing clients. Never trust a client to decide whether it hit an enemy, completed a hack, grabbed a hostage, or won the match.
14.2 Latency and prediction: responsive local movement prediction; server correction; interpolation for remote players; lag-compensated hit validation where appropriate; time-limited rewind; server validation of interaction distance and timing; reliable handling of major state changes. Test hostage interactions and climbing under simulated latency.
14.3 Matchmaking and lobbies: public matchmaking; private lobbies; invite-based joining; team balancing; ready checks; match cancellation handling; reconnection support; post-match rematch flow. Start with a simple lobby and session flow; keep the session layer modular. Do not assume a backend or hosting provider is configured.
14.4 Security and anti-cheat: validate movement speed, traversal anchors, interaction range, firing rate, ammunition, gadget charges, cooldowns, objective completion, hostage state transitions, damage and team membership. No admin functions or server secrets exposed to clients. No credentials in source control.

15. GAME BALANCE (initial test values)
Team size 2v2. Round 7 minutes. Primary objective interaction about 10–15 seconds. Ghost health low enough to punish sustained gunfire. Warden health higher, but still vulnerable to valid stealth takedowns. Ghost sprint faster than Warden movement. Ghost climbing exclusive to authored traversal routes. Warden flashlight unlimited for the first prototype. Gadgets limited by charges or cooldowns. Hostage grab requires positioning and a valid server-approved interaction. Takedowns short, interruptible only under explicitly defined rules.
Balance questions: Can Wardens defend without camping a single doorway? Can Ghosts distract without an unavoidable mechanic? Can a Warden detect a careful Ghost using sound and lighting? Can two Ghosts coordinate a simultaneous distraction? Can one Warden rescue a hostage without automatically losing the objective? Can Wardens survive a ceiling ambush if attentive? Can both teams recover after a mistake? Are vertical routes useful without making conventional movement irrelevant? Record match data and adjust with repeated playtests.

16. TECHNICAL IMPLEMENTATION AND CODE ORGANIZATION
Modular architecture. Suggested modules: Core (match rules, team management, player state, round management, scorekeeping, game configuration); Ghost (movement, traversal, stealth visibility, gadgets, hacking, takedown); Warden (weapon, flashlight, sound detection, defensive gadgets, first-person presentation); Interaction (interface, server validation, contextual prompts, progress tracking, cancellation); Hostage (state machine, grab validation, synchronized animation, rescue logic, voice routing); Multiplayer (replication policies, session management, matchmaking adapter, reconnection, network diagnostics); UI (main menu, lobby, HUD, scoreboard, settings, accessibility); Audio (footsteps, spatial audio, ambience, voice integration, mix settings). Reusable interfaces, documented classes, replicated variables, messages, states and dependencies. No single enormous file containing all game logic.

17. PERFORMANCE AND QUALITY TARGETS
Stable 60 FPS on the agreed minimum PC specification; higher refresh rates on suitable hardware; consistent frame pacing; low input latency; predictable server tick; efficient replication; controlled memory; reliable level loading. Profile game thread, render thread, GPU, animation, physics, audio, network traffic and server performance. Scalable options for shadows, global illumination, reflections, effects, textures, view distance, anti-aliasing, upscaling and volumetric lighting. Do not claim the game is optimized without measured results.

18. DEVELOPMENT ROADMAP (original; see CLAUDE.md for the current order)
Phase 1 Graybox: one small graybox map; two playable teams; third-person Ghost and first-person Warden movement; basic climbing and ledge hanging; basic weapons and damage; one hackable terminal; a working round timer; local and two-client network test. Acceptance: two players connect to a dedicated test server, occupy opposing roles, interact with the objective, and complete a match.
Phase 2 Core asymmetry: Shadow Drop; flashlights; basic sound detection; stealth visibility; one Ghost gadget; one Warden gadget; objective contesting; team coordination; basic scoreboard.
Phase 3 Hostage mechanics: server-validated grab; synchronized hostage animations; restricted hostage movement; voice interaction routing; voice filter presets; rescue and escape rules; takedown and interruption rules. Acceptance: coherent under simulated latency, interruptible or resolvable by the rules.
Phase 4 Art and atmosphere: final-quality environment assets; PBR materials; lighting; flashlight effects; character models; animation polish; environmental audio; interactive lights; initial UI. Acceptance: coherent visual identity, readable in low light.
Phase 5 Online play: dedicated server packaging; session creation and joining; private lobbies; public matchmaking integration; disconnect handling; reconnection; server validation; basic abuse protection. Acceptance: real players on separate machines complete full matches.
Phase 6 Alpha testing: latency; packet loss; map exploits; spawn fairness; objective balance; flashlight performance; voice chat reliability; climbing edge cases; hostage desync; minimum-hardware performance.
Phase 7 Release preparation: improved matchmaking; tutorial; additional maps; additional loadouts; progression and cosmetics; server deployment and monitoring; crash reporting; privacy settings; accessibility; final performance optimization.
Do not begin large-scale content production until the core 2v2 experience is fun and technically reliable.

19. HOW THE AI DEVELOPER MUST WORK
Inspect the existing project first. Work in small, verifiable milestones. For every milestone: explain the objective; identify the systems and files; implement actual gameplay logic; provide complete source files; explain setup, input mappings and asset dependencies; provide build and launch instructions; run available compile checks and tests; report failures honestly; state what works, what is incomplete, and what comes next. Do not claim multiplayer, voice chat, matchmaking, animation, or server deployment is functional unless implemented and tested. If a feature depends on unavailable external services, create a documented integration boundary and a working local alternative. Never silently replace online multiplayer with a single-player simulation. Prioritize a playable vertical slice over large amounts of incomplete code.

20. FIRST TASK (vertical slice, largely delivered by M1 to M7)
Compact graybox facility with one ground floor, one upper service level, one overhead traversal route, two data terminals, several controllable lights, multiple entrances and alternate routes, Ghost and Warden spawn areas. Two-player online connectivity; dedicated authoritative server; third-person Ghost; first-person Warden; basic climbing and ledge hanging; functional Warden flashlight; server-authoritative weapon damage; basic hacking; round timer; win and loss conditions; simple HUD. Then Shadow Drop, sound detection and hostage interaction, in that order.
Final goal: a polished, original, online asymmetric stealth game in which two Ghosts infiltrate a dark research facility while two Wardens defend it, with parkour, tactical gunplay, sound-based hunting, vertical ambushes, hacking, and tense hostage interactions. Build the foundation correctly. Make it playable. Test it. Then make it beautiful.
